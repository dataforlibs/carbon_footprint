Country sensitivity dashboard

How padding, snapshots, frequency and first-fit rules change country lifepaths and fold counts. All numbers are pregenerated; nothing is fitted in this page.
About this dashboard

This dashboard accompanies the paper "Sigmoid-parameterized lifepaths…". The paper tracks each country's research output as a logistic (sigmoid) curve, refitted over time as new publications arrive. Each refit gives an inflection point, x₀ (the estimated moment of fastest growth), and a phase: acceleration, inflection, deceleration or deactivated. A fold happens when a new fit moves x₀ earlier (backward) or later (forward) by more than one window step while x₀ stays inside the observed period. This page asks whether folds, and the lifepaths themselves, depend on analysis choices. All results are pregenerated; nothing is refitted in your browser.

What you can change. Padding: none, or 100 zero-months before the data begin. Snapshot rule: A (aggregated) sums publications into 3-, 6- or 12-month buckets and fits the curve to those buckets, so the window size also sets the resolution of the data. B (monthly snapshot) always fits the monthly series and only records the result at the end of each window, so the window size changes how often results are recorded, not the data. Frequency: a new fit every 3, 6 or 12 months. Minimum documents: a country enters once it has 0–5 publications. Minimum months: a country enters after a set number of months since its first publication. A fit at any window uses all data up to that window, so the two minimum rules only choose which windows count and never change a fit.

What you see. (1) Summary tiles compared with the baseline setting. (2) Aggregate results: countries analyzed at each window, backward, forward and total folds per year (optionally saturation phase only), the distribution of final inflection years, and the final phase mix. (3) Individual lifepaths: pick a country to see its lifepath and how its x₀ moves across windows. (4) Cluster tests for Paris 2015–16 and COVID 2019–21.

How to read the cluster tests. For each setting and fold type, a permutation test asks how often the same number of folds, placed at random years, would put at least as many inside the event window. Because many settings are tested, the page also reports how many results would be significant by chance alone, p-values corrected for that (Benjamini-Hochberg), and a placebo: the share of other windows of the same width that hold as many folds. In these data no setting shows significant clustering at either event, before or after correction. The 2015 backward-fold bar is a real count, but similar counts occur in other years.

Reading the numbers. Fits use the same logistic model and caps as the paper: x₀ is capped at 1.3 in fitting, and a fold requires x₀ ≤ 1 in both windows. Windows are counted from each country's first publication. The data run to July 2023. One window step is about six months at 6-month frequency.

4 · Do folds really cluster around Paris and COVID?

The fold histograms above label Paris (2015–16) and COVID (2019–21) with a permutation p-value: how often would the same number of folds, placed at random years, put at least as many inside that window. This section repeats the test across every scenario, so you can see whether any choice of parameters produces a clustering that is not an accident.

Strongest 15 results across all scenarios

Fold types with at least 20 folds only. BH q is the p-value corrected for having looked at many scenarios (Benjamini-Hochberg). Placebo is the share of other windows of the same width, anywhere in 2007–2023, that hold at least as many folds: a small value means the event window stands out, a large one means ordinary years look just as busy. Each row is one scenario, with its total, backward (Back.) and forward (Fwd.) folds overall, and inside Paris 2015–16 and COVID 2019–21, each with its own permutation p-value (– where there are no folds to test). Rows are ranked by the smallest p-value among that scenario's tests, so the strongest test is often a Paris one; the COVID p-values are in the same row.
